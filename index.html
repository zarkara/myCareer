Most people don't have a resume problem.
They have a recall problem.

Twenty years of work compresses, in memory, into six bullets that could describe anyone. The projects blur, the numbers go missing, and the decision you are proudest of turns into a sentence with no detail in it, because the detail was never written down anywhere.

It starts from the other end

Instead of asking what you achieved, it describes the terrain you were probably standing on and lets you recognise what you actually did there. If you ran a SOC 2 program, you almost certainly did ISO 27001 control work and never filed it under that name. Recognition is far easier than recall, and a long career is exactly where recall fails worst.

Then it writes the resume, the LinkedIn profile and the executive letters from that record, and refuses to let any document claim more than the record supports.

Three things it does that a resume tool does not

—

Nothing it suggests becomes a fact

Every prompt is a candidate until you confirm it in your own words, with a particular it did not hand you: a person, a date, an artifact, an argument. Agreeing with its sentence is not the same as remembering yours.

—

Three claims, never one

You did it, you scoped it and handed it off, or you enabled a team to do it. Those are different things, and letting one drift into another is how people come apart in a reference check.

—

A number you cannot source gets left out

Not softened into "significant improvement". Left out, and listed separately so you know what you would need to find to use it.
